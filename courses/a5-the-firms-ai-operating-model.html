The firm's AI operating model: build, buy, or wrap

"Should we build our own AI?" is the wrong first question - it starts with the technology and works backward to a justification. The right first question is "what do we uniquely know, and how does AI multiply it?" This session reads the reference cases honestly - McKinsey's Lilli, BCG's internal tools, PwC's buy-and-resell play, the EY and KPMG platform bets - then translates them to a firm your size: three realistic tiers, a decision test for each, and a review date on whatever you choose.

Part 0

The wrong question and the right one

Every partner meeting on AI eventually produces the sentence "maybe we should build our own." Sometimes it is right. Usually it is envy of a headline. The firms in today's reference cases spent anywhere from a subscription fee to two billion dollars, and the honest pattern across all of them is not "build wins" or "buy wins" - it is that the value tracked what the firm uniquely knew, not what it built. This session gives you the cases with their labels on, then a three-tier model sized for firms that are not McKinsey, which is nearly all of them.

Part 1 · covers the reference firms

The reference cases, honestly read 8 min live

Four strategies are visible from the outside, and each teaches one thing. Read them as signals about where value sits, not as templates to copy - the budgets alone should tell you why.

LiveWhat Lilli actually proves4 min▶

McKinsey's Lilli - launched 2023, named for Lillian Dombrowski, the firm's first professional woman hire - is the most instructive case because it is the least about the model. Lilli searches 40+ internal knowledge sources and 100k+ documents: decades of engagements, methods, and expert profiles nobody else has. Roughly 72-75% of the firm's ~43,000 people use it, at 500k+ prompts a month, and McKinsey claims about 30% time saved on information gathering - their claim, and label it that way when you repeat it.

Strip the scale away and the design decision remains: McKinsey did not build a better model. It pointed a model at the one asset no competitor can copy - its own accumulated knowledge. That is the transferable lesson, and it transfers down to a twelve-person boutique: the question is never "can we build Lilli?" but "what is our version of the thing Lilli searches?"

Real worldPwC ran the opposite play and it is just as instructive: buy ChatGPT Enterprise for 100k+ employees, then become OpenAI's first reseller - the firm as distribution channel. PwC monetized adoption itself: learn the tools by running on them, then sell that competence to clients. A boutique's version is smaller but real - the firm that runs its own AI operating model credibly is more convincing selling AI advice.
Self-studyHow to read firm announcements without being played2 min▶

Every number in this part came from the firm itself or from press coverage of the firm's statements. That does not make them false - it makes them marketing-shaped. BCG's ~90% employee usage and its Deckster and GENE tools are press-reported BCG statements. EY's US$1.4B and KPMG's US$2B Microsoft commitment are announced investment figures, not audited outcomes. None of these firms publishes the number you actually want: profit impact per dollar spent. Read announcements the way a4 taught you to read an AI draft - plausible, fluent, and unverified until someone accountable shows the source. The discipline you apply to their numbers is the discipline clients will apply to yours.

Part 2 · covers the three tiers

The boutique translation: wrap, assemble, build 8 min live

Most firms are not McKinsey, and pretending otherwise is how AI budgets die. The realistic ladder has three rungs, and every firm starts on the first one whether it admits it or not.

TierWhat it isTime + costDecision test - choose it when...
1 · WrapEnterprise subscriptions + firm playbooks and prompt libraries + a written policy with an ownerWeeks · low costAlways. This is where every firm starts; the only failure mode is not doing it deliberately.
2 · AssembleRetrieval over the firm's own past work - the Lilli pattern at boutique scaleMonths · moderateYour past engagements are genuinely reusable AND you have written the knowledge-governance rule first: which past-client material may enter your own knowledge base, and anonymized how.
3 · BuildCustom tools or agents for one workflowQuarters · highA repeatable, differentiating workflow exists and you can name it in one sentence. If you cannot name it, the honest answer is "none yet."
LiveTier 2 is a governance decision wearing a technology costume4 min▶

The assemble tier tempts every firm with a file server full of old decks, and the temptation is legitimate - retrieval over your own past work is exactly what made Lilli valuable, and the knowledge base matters far more than the model you put in front of it. But notice what you are proposing: making two hundred past engagements searchable by everyone in the firm. Client A's confidential pricing study is now one query away from the team serving Client A's competitor.

So the prerequisite is not a vector database - it is a sentence: which past-client material may enter the knowledge base, under what anonymization, and who decides. Write that rule before the pilot, not after the incident. It is the same confidentiality rail from a3, pointed inward: the Samsung lesson applies to your own knowledge base just as much as to a vendor's chat window. A firm that cannot write this rule in a week is not ready for tier 2, and that is useful information, not failure.

Self-studyThe moat question2 min▶

Run the copyability test on everything in your operating model. Prompt libraries: copyable in an afternoon - any competitor with the same tools converges on similar prompts. Policies: copyable - and should be, since good policy is not a secret. Enterprise subscriptions: identical to everyone else's by definition. Now the other column: your 200 past engagements are not copyable. Neither are your benchmarks, your sector relationships, or the pattern library in your partners' heads. The moat was never the AI layer; it is what the AI layer gets pointed at. Which is why tier 2 - done with the governance rule written first - is where a boutique's durable advantage lives, and why tier 1 alone, however necessary, differentiates nobody.

Part 3 · covers adoption

Making it stick 5 min live

Operating models fail at adoption, not selection. And adoption is a leadership behavior problem, not a training problem - the study's floor-raising result means most of the gains are available on day one with the wrap tier, so the constraint is whether your people actually change how they work.

LiveThree rituals and one budget truth4 min▶

Three rituals carry adoption. Usage reviews: look at whether the tools are actually used, by team, monthly - not to punish, but because silence tells you where the playbook does not fit the work. Playbook rituals: the prompt library is a living document with a named owner; teams add what worked and retire what did not, or it dies as a PDF. Celebrating catches: when a reviewer catches an AI-invented fact before it reaches a client, praise it publicly - that is the a4 review discipline paying out, and what gets celebrated gets repeated.

And the budget truth that reframes every pricing conversation with your own partners: tool spend is trivial next to partner review time. The subscriptions for an entire boutique cost less than a few days of one partner's time. If the operating-model debate is consuming partner hours arguing about tool costs, the debate itself is the expensive part. Decide, write it down, put a review date on it, and move.

Real worldThe celebrate-the-catch ritual has a grim counterexample: Deloitte Australia's 2025 partial refund happened because fabricated references and a misattributed court quote - errors Deloitte attributed to generative AI use it later disclosed - reached a government client uncaught. Every catch your reviewers make is that headline not happening to you. Price the ritual accordingly.
Everyone decides

Choose your tier 🧭 ★ 18 min · everyone works

Leave the session with the decision drafted, not deferred. One page, six steps, and a date when you will revisit it.

Inventory what your firm uniquely knows. Methods, benchmarks, past engagements, sector depth, the partner who has seen forty restructurings. Ten minutes, bullet points, no modesty and no inflation.

Score the three tiers against that inventory. Wrap multiplies individual work; assemble multiplies the inventory itself; build multiplies one workflow. Which of your assets does each tier actually touch?

Write the wrap shopping list. Which tools, which tiers (enterprise, always - a3's rail), and the name of the policy owner. This list is your floor regardless of what else you choose.

If assemble tempts you, write the knowledge-governance rule first. One line: which past-client material may enter the knowledge base, anonymized how, decided by whom. No rule, no tier 2.

Name the single workflow that would justify build - or write "none yet." "None yet" is a respectable answer and a cheap one. A vague workflow is the expensive answer.

Put a review date on the decision. Six months out. The frontier moves, the vendors move, and an operating model without a review date quietly becomes a relic nobody is allowed to question.

✦ If you finish early Stress-test your tier choice against the moat question: for each element, ask "could a rival firm copy this in a quarter?" Anything that survives is strategy; everything else is hygiene. Both matter, but only one deserves partner-meeting time.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What makes McKinsey's Lilli valuable, on the honest reading of the case?

The model is replaceable; the decades of engagements, methods, and expert knowledge it searches are not. That is why the boutique translation of Lilli is "make your own past work retrievable," not "train a model."

2 · The three realistic tiers for a firm that is not McKinsey are...

Wrap is weeks and low cost - every firm starts there. Assemble is the Lilli pattern at boutique scale, gated by a written knowledge-governance rule. Build is justified only by a repeatable, differentiating workflow you can name.

3 · Which of these is NOT a durable moat for a consulting firm?

Prompts and policies are necessary hygiene, but they converge across firms almost immediately. What compounds is what cannot be copied: your past work, your benchmarks, your relationships - the things tier 2 makes retrievable.
