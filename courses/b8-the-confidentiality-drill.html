The confidentiality drill: the gate that outranks craft

You have known the rule since session 1: client material stays out of untrusted tools. This session makes it a reflex. The lab's gate does not negotiate - a draft that would score 87 on craft caps at 15 the moment it names names, because that is exactly how it works in real life, except the cap is your firm's reputation. Today: the five kinds of leakage, the anonymization craft that preserves your analysis, and what to do when the gate fires anyway.

Part 0

Why craft cannot buy the gate open

Every measure the scorer has taught you - answer first, quantified, so-whats, anchors - rewards putting MORE specifics on the page. This session teaches the one place that instinct inverts. The note in Cadence's data room from b1 - the large financial-services prospect, with names and a contract figure attached - is sitting in your notes, and week 5's pricing analysis genuinely benefits from it. The lab's gate already knows its contents, and the moment any of them enters a draft bound for an untrusted tool, no amount of craft matters. 87 on prose alone; capped at 15. The order of operations is the lesson: confidentiality first, craft second, always.

Part 1 · covers the taxonomy

What counts as leakage 7 min live

"Do not paste confidential stuff" fails as a rule because nobody thinks their paste is the confidential one. A taxonomy works better: five categories, each with a reason it identifies and a version that does not.

LiveThe taxonomy, row by row4 min▶
CategoryWhy it identifiesThe anonymized version
Client identitynames, logos, and unmistakable descriptions ("the largest AI note-taker with a 5-seat cap") all point at one company"a B2B SaaS client with strong team-level pull"
Peoplenames and emails obviously; but role-plus-context identifies too - there is only one CRO of any given company"a senior commercial stakeholder"
Commercial termscontract values, pricing under NDA, and deal stage are the facts rivals want most - and often unique enough to identify both parties"a contract in the low millions, late-stage"
Client's-client materialthe Meridian Bank problem: Cadence's prospect is TWO trust relationships deep - the prospect never consented to your tooling at all"a major financial-services prospect"
Internal materialboard decks, unreleased numbers, and pre-announcement plans - material whose whole value is that it is not public yetquote the shape ("board-level growth target"), never the artifact

The Samsung story is the pattern to memorize: in spring 2023, Samsung permitted ChatGPT internally, and within about twenty days employees had pasted confidential source code (twice) and an internal meeting transcript into it - by May, generative AI was banned on company devices. Permitted tool, helpful intent, three leaks in twenty days. Nobody was malicious; everybody was productive. That is exactly why the rule has to live in your hands, not in a policy document.

Real worldNote what the Samsung incident was made of: not spies, not negligence - engineers trying to fix code faster and summarize a meeting. The consulting version is worse in one specific way: the material you would paste belongs to your CLIENT, and in the Meridian case, to your client's client. Trust two relationships deep is the easiest thing in the world to spend and the hardest to buy back.
Self-studyWhy "roles plus context" counts as a name2 min▶

The subtle row in the taxonomy is people. Everyone knows not to paste "Elena Voss"; fewer notice that "the CRO of our financial-services prospect, who joined from a payments company last year" is the same disclosure wearing a trench coat. Identification is a property of the COMBINATION, not the tokens: role + industry + timing can single out one human as surely as a surname. The working test: search your anonymized sentence in your head - how many real people could it describe? If the answer is "one," it is a name. This is the same logic privacy engineers call re-identification, and consultants get no exemption from arithmetic.

Part 2 · covers the craft

Anonymization that preserves the analysis 8 min live

Anonymization done badly deletes the analysis along with the identity - "a company had a problem" helps no one. Done well, it replaces identity and keeps the analytical shape: every relationship, magnitude, and constraint your reasoning needs survives the rewrite.

LiveThe craft, and the architecture underneath it5 min▶

The move: replace identity, keep the analytical shape. "Meridian Bank's $4.2M contract" becomes "a major financial-services prospect in late-stage talks (contract in the low millions)" - the size, stage, and stakes your pricing analysis needs all survive; the identity does not. Then run the two tests: could a rival identify the party from your anonymized version? Could your analysis still run on what remains? Fail the first, you leaked; fail the second, you over-scrubbed.

Role-based references: "the client's CRO" becomes "a senior commercial stakeholder" when even the role identifies - and at a company with one CRO, it always does. Numbers: round or band them when the exact figure is itself identifying; a $4.2M contract is findable, "low millions" is not, and your margin math almost never needed the second decimal anyway.

The second layer is architectural: enterprise-tier tools. The clean rule from the course map: consumer tiers of the major vendors may train on your conversations unless you opt out; commercial, enterprise, and API tiers do not train by default - a contractual guarantee, not a vibe. And then anonymize anyway: defense in depth, because terms change, tabs get mixed up, and screenshots outlive settings pages.

Real worldTerms do change - the course map notes Anthropic's consumer terms changed in 2025, after which consumer-tier chats train unless the user opts out, while commercial tiers kept no-training by default. Anyone whose habit was "this vendor never trains on chats" woke up with an outdated habit. Habits anchored to a vendor's current settings age; habits anchored to "anonymize before it leaves my notes" do not. That is the whole argument for the drill.
Self-studyThe drill for teams: the forbidden-list2 min▶

Individual reflexes decay under deadline pressure; shared artifacts do not. The team version of this session is a forbidden-list per engagement, written at kickoff, taped to the wall (or pinned in the channel): every client name, person, code name, contract figure, and internal artifact that must never enter a prompt - with the approved anonymization alongside each. Two virtues: writing it forces the team to decide what is sensitive BEFORE anyone is in a hurry, and checking a draft against a list is mechanical enough to do at 11pm, which is when leaks happen. The lab's gate is exactly this - a forbidden-list with a scorer attached. Steal the pattern.

Part 3 · covers incident posture

When the gate fires anyway 4 min live

Someone on your team will eventually paste the wrong thing - the Samsung employees were trying to be productive, not careless. The posture that limits the damage: no-blame reporting beats hidden leaks, every time.

LiveThe four steps, and the honest severity scale4 min▶

The four steps, in order: (1) stop - no more material into that session, no cleanup prompts, no "please forget that"; (2) tell the engagement lead - immediately, before the awkwardness compounds; (3) assess what tier and tool received it - enterprise with a no-training contract, or a consumer tier that may train on it; (4) document - what entered, when, which tool, which tier, who has been told.

One honest paragraph, because severity differs and pretending otherwise breeds hiding: a leak into an enterprise no-training tool is a policy breach to log - the contractual layer held, the behavioral layer failed, fix the habit. A leak into a consumer tool is an incident to escalate - the material may now sit in training pipelines you cannot recall. Different severity, same reporting duty. A team that punishes the report gets fewer reports, not fewer leaks - and the leak the lead never hears about is the one that ends up in the news.

Everyone runs the gate

The gate ▶ ★ 18 min · everyone works

Two buttons, one lesson. The lab loads a genuinely well-written draft - and the gate ignores every point of its craft. The score is always computed live from the text on the page; the leaks it lists are really there.

1 · Press "Load the leaky draft" and read it. Craft-wise it is a 90s draft: answer first, quantified everywhere, so-whats on every bullet. Your b5-trained eye should admire it. That is the trap working.

2 · Read the alarm. Capped at 15 regardless - 87 on prose alone. The gate lists exactly what it found: the client's client named, a real person, an unreleased contract value, board material. Four categories from Part 1, live on your screen.

3 · Say the rule out loud. Any ONE of these in a prompt to an untrusted tool is a Samsung-shaped incident. Not the combination - any one.

4 · Press "🧹 Anonymize, then use". Gate open, 87 restored. Read what changed: "a major financial-services prospect in late-stage talks" carries the analytical weight without the identity. The craft score was there all along - the gate just outranks it.

5 · Write your own anonymization of one real client detail you know - on paper, not in the lab. Run the two tests: rival identification, analysis survival. This is the rep that makes it a reflex.

6 · Say the takeaway sentence together: anonymize-then-prompt is the habit, enterprise-tier is the architecture, and you need both.

✦ If you finish early Start your engagement forbidden-list right now - it is homework anyway. Five minutes gets the obvious rows: client names, people, figures, code names, internal artifacts. The approved anonymization column is the part your teammates will actually thank you for.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · The five leakage categories are...

The taxonomy beats "don't paste confidential stuff" because it is checkable row by row. The subtlest rows: people identified by role-plus-context, and the Meridian problem - material two trust relationships deep, from a party who never consented to your tooling.

2 · Anonymization that preserves the analysis means...

"A major financial-services prospect in late-stage talks (contract in the low millions)" keeps size, stage, and stakes - the reasoning survives. Two tests: could a rival identify the party? could your analysis still run? Initials fail the first test; total deletion fails the second.

3 · Why anonymize even inside enterprise-tier tools with no-training contracts?

Enterprise-tier is the architecture; anonymize-then-prompt is the habit. The contractual layer is real (commercial/enterprise/API tiers do not train by default) - and you keep the behavioral layer anyway, because the layer you control is the one that never changes terms on you.
