Week 5 at Northbridge. The storyline scored 100 last session, and now it needs pages - the deck is the storyline wearing clothes, and AI will dress it in minutes. Which is exactly the problem. This session is about the difference between a deck AI accelerated and a deck AI wrote: the first is your storyline, faster; the second is the average of every strategy deck the model has ever seen, about no company in particular. Part 0

Why this session exists

Every previous week produced something AI could not fake: logged sources, counted interviews, a governing thought with your signature under it. Week 5 produces the artifact AI fakes best - a deck. Fluent slides are now free, which means fluency has stopped being evidence of anything. What still separates a Northbridge deck from a plausible one is measurable: the client's actual numbers on every page, one committed message per slide, and exhibits that prove their titles. The scorer from b5 already prices all three, and today it meets its natural enemy: a beautiful draft about nothing.

Part 1 · covers the mapping

From pyramid to pages 7 min live

The good news about deck week: the hard thinking is already done. B5's pyramid maps one-to-one onto the deck - the governing thought becomes the title slide and executive summary, each support argument becomes a section, and each evidence point becomes exactly one slide carrying exactly one message. The deck is not a new document; it is the same pyramid, paginated.

LiveThe mapping, rule by rule4 min▶

Three rules turn the pyramid into pages. Governing thought = title slide + executive summary. The answer travels first in the deck exactly as it did in the summary - a partner who reads only page one leaves with the recommendation. Each support argument = a section. Demand, economics, window: three dividers, three chapters, in the pyramid's order. Each evidence point = one slide with one message, and the action title IS the message: "62% of trial teams hit the seat cap in 3 weeks" is a title; the slide beneath it exists to prove that sentence and nothing else.

The elevator test from b5 becomes a page-flip test here: read the titles in sequence, ignoring every body. If the titles alone carry the storyline, the deck works. If a title merely names a topic - "Retention analysis" - the page is freight without an address. And exhibits earn their place by proving the title; anything on the page that does not prove the title is decoration, and decoration is deleted.

Real worldThis is the deck craft Barbara Minto's pyramid was built to feed - she codified it at McKinsey from 1966 onward, and the firm still teaches action titles as the pyramid's visible surface. The craft outlived every slide-software transition since 1985 because it is not about software; it is about how a reader with ninety seconds actually reads. AI changed who types the first draft. It did not change the ninety seconds.
Self-studySizing the deck before drafting it2 min▶

A useful pre-draft habit: count the pyramid before opening any tool. The Cadence pyramid has one governing thought, three arguments, and six evidence points that survived b4's quality gate - so the readout deck wants roughly seven content slides plus dividers, and anything much longer is padding wearing a suit. Sizing from the pyramid also gives you the AI brief for free: seven titles, each with its number, is a checklist a model can draft against and a human can verify in minutes. Decks grow when they are drafted before they are counted.

Part 2 · covers the anti-lever

The regression-to-average trap 8 min live

Ask a model to "write the deck" cold and it does exactly what it is built to do: produce the most probable strategy deck - the average of every strategy deck it has seen. The result is fluent, confident, structurally immaculate, and about no company on earth. This is not a bug to be prompted away; it is the mechanism. Your client's specifics live in your engagement, not in the training data, so a cold draft literally cannot contain them.

LiveThe tell-tale signs of a slop deck4 min▶

Slop has a signature, and once you see it you cannot unsee it:

TellWhat it looks likeWhy it happens
Topic titles"Market overview" · "Strategic considerations"a topic title claims nothing, so it can never be wrong - the safest possible average
Weasel clusters"holistic", "robust", "in today's dynamic landscape"the highest-probability filler across every deck ever written converges here
Zero client numbersno 62%, no 210, no 94% vs 71%, no $30the client's specifics are not in the training data - the model cannot know what it was never fed
Fits-any-company advice"invest in innovation and align stakeholders"regression to the mean of strategy: advice that fits every company helps none

Notice that the b5 measures are, collectively, a slop detector. Quantified support and client anchors are the sharpest blades: a slop deck scores ZERO on anchors, because "Cadence", "seat cap", "procurement", and "$30" simply do not appear - and near zero on quantified support, because the average deck hedges where numbers would commit. The lab below makes this concrete: the fluent full-AI draft scores 8 of 100. Read that draft aloud before you see the score; it genuinely flows.

Real worldThe mechanism is documented, not folklore: the jagged-frontier study found AI-assisted consultants' outputs homogenized - answers converged on similar ideas across hundreds of professionals. Convergence is fine when you want the consensus view; it is fatal when the client is paying for the one deck that is about THEM. The homogenization finding is the anti-lever's evidence base: the more of the deck you delegate cold, the more average it becomes.
Self-studyThe working division of labor2 min▶

The fix is a contract, not a boycott: AI proposes structure and first-pass language FROM YOUR FACTS. Feed it the source log and the storyline - "here are the seven titles with their numbers; draft the body bullets for slide 3, each ending in its consequence" - and it is a superb accelerant. Never ask it to "write the deck about enterprise SaaS pricing" cold; that prompt has no access to anything that makes the deck yours. The tools in both experiments below are identical. The only difference is who supplies the facts, the so-whats, and the signature - and that difference is 92 points.

Real worldThe firms furthest into AI adoption run exactly this contract at scale. BCG - which reports, in press-covered statements, that around 90% of its employees use AI and that it built internal tools including Deckster for slide drafting - still ships decks with consultant ownership of content; the tooling accelerates production, it does not replace the engagement's facts. An internal drafting tool with the firm's knowledge behind it is still downstream of someone doing weeks 2-4 properly.
Part 3 · covers the page-level craft

Exhibits and the one-message rule 5 min live

Every slide answers one question for its reader: "so what should I believe from this page?" The action title states the answer; the exhibit proves it; everything else goes. Two working tests keep the rule enforceable at 11pm.

LiveThe two tests, and where AI helps3 min▶

Test one - the "and" test: if the action title needs "and" twice, the page is carrying two messages, and it is two slides. Splitting is free; a muddled page is not. Test two - exhibit honesty: the chart shows the number that proves the title, at the size the argument deserves. Two bars, 94 vs 71, beats a 12-line table every time the message is "converted accounts compound" - the table is more data and less proof.

AI's role at the page level: it is genuinely good at suggesting exhibit FORMS - "what is the cleanest way to show a retention gap between two cohorts?" is a fine prompt, and "two bars" is a fine answer. The numbers inside the exhibit come from week 2-3's logged work, full stop. Form from the model, facts from the log, message from you - the one-message rule holds the three together on every page.

Self-studyWhere decoration goes to live: the appendix2 min▶

"Decoration is deleted" does not mean the supporting material dies - it moves. The appendix is the one-message rule's pressure valve, and it has its own discipline:

  • The 12-line retention table goes to the appendix, referenced from the two-bar slide - present for the analyst who asks, absent from the argument's path.
  • Methodology and the source log live there too: when a client asks "where does the 62% come from?", the answer is one page-flip away, which is exactly the confidence b3's discipline was built to buy.
  • Nothing enters the main flow from the appendix without earning a title of its own. If a backup page keeps getting pulled forward in rehearsal, it was a message all along - promote it, give it an action title, and count the deck again.

AI is useful here in the least glamorous way: "list every exhibit in this draft that does not directly prove its slide's title" is a fine demotion-candidate prompt. The demotion decision, like every other one this week, stays with you.

Everyone presses both buttons

The slop experiment ▶ ★ 18 min · everyone works

Two buttons, two contracts, one scorer. The first drafts the Cadence exec summary the way a cold "write the deck" prompt would; the second uses the same tools with the consultant supplying the facts. The score is always computed live from the text on the page - the measures cannot be charmed.

Press "✨ Let AI write it all" - and read the draft aloud. Genuinely, aloud. It flows beautifully: confident verbs, balanced clauses, executive rhythm. Fluency is the product the model optimizes; that is why the trap catches good people.

Now read the score: 8 of 100. Walk the table: client anchors 0/15 - Cadence, seats, procurement, $30 appear nowhere; quantified support 0/20 - not one claim carries a number; the weasel count is high and each hit is priced. The measures cannot be charmed by fluency, which is precisely what they are for.

Name the trap out loud. Point at any sentence of the slop draft and say it: this paragraph fits every company on earth, which is exactly why it helps none. Regression to the average, felt in your own reading voice.

Press "🤝 AI drafts, consultant owns" - same tools, different contract. The client's numbers, the so-whats, and the signature are supplied by the consultant; the model drafts from those facts. Score: 100. The 92-point gap is not model quality - it is who supplied the facts.

Write the one-sentence rule you will keep. The canonical form: feed facts in, never ask for facts out. Write it in your own words and pin it where you draft - b10's capstone asks whether you kept it.

Bonus: audit your own last deck's title slide. Find the weasel - most title slides have at least one "robust" or "holistic" hiding in plain sight. Rewrite the sentence with a number where the weasel was, and feel the b5 ladder doing its work outside the lab.

✦ If you finish early Take the slop draft's most confident sentence and try to falsify it. You cannot - it commits to nothing checkable. Then take the owned draft's opening line: $30/seat, Q1, 210 accounts - falsifiable in three places. Checkability is what the client is paying for.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · The one-message-per-slide rule means...

The title states the message ("62% of trial teams hit the seat cap"), the exhibit proves it, and decoration is deleted. If the title needs "and" twice, it is two slides. Titles read in sequence must carry the whole storyline.

2 · The lab's full-AI draft scores 8 of 100 because...

Client anchors 0/15 and quantified support 0/20 do most of the damage. A cold prompt regresses to the average of every strategy deck the model has seen, and the average deck contains no Cadence, no 62%, no $30. The measures cannot be charmed by flow.

3 · The working division of labor for deck week is...

Same tools in both lab experiments; the only difference is who supplies the facts - and that difference is 92 points. Feed it the source log and the storyline and it accelerates superbly. Ask it to "write the deck about X" cold and you get the average of everything.
